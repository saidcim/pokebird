PokeBird Belgrad Forest Field Test, 31 August 2026

The device left the lab for the first time and ran in Belgrad Forest from start to finish: it powered up, listened, and printed species names on its screen. Every previous check had been made against a held-out test set or at a desk with controlled playback, so this is the first evidence that the recognition chain works through the device's own microphone, at its own distance, inside real forest noise.

It correctly identified Common Chaffinch, Hooded Crow, European Robin and woodpeckers, all four inside the model's 178-species Istanbul list. The crow result is encouraging because its closest relative, the Carrion Crow, was deliberately excluded when the list was built, making confusion unlikely by construction. Which woodpecker was heard went unrecorded, and the model carries six, so that one counts as a family-level hit rather than a species-level one.

The only concrete problem was that touch input is not fluid. This is not a new defect: fifteen deliberate touches at the desk had produced nine registrations, an acceptance rate near sixty percent, and the field has now independently confirmed that measurement. A suspect was noted in the source at the time, namely that the button strip ends around raw coordinate 368 while the acceptance threshold sits at 370, leaving roughly two raw units of margin; the eight-hundred-millisecond maximum press duration is a second candidate. The diagnostic that separates the two is already compiled into the firmware and has never been run.

The outing left no data behind. No serial log was started, no parallel phone audio was recorded, and the screen was not captured; the device stores nothing itself, having neither an SD card nor a clock. What we have is therefore an impression rather than a number. Seeing no wrong species means none were observed, not that none occurred, and the four correct calls rest on a judgement by ear. They are a signal worth measuring, not a claim of accuracy.

Turning this into numbers needs only what is already in the repository: a logger that timestamps every decision the device prints to its serial port, and a tool that aligns that log against a BirdNET analysis of audio recorded in parallel, the two tied together by a hand clap at each end of the walk. Before the next outing the logger should be rehearsed at home, the touch diagnostic should be captured, and the uncommitted power-button code should be verified on battery. A separate morning spent recording Istanbul's own noise would also replace a negative class drawn entirely from a foreign dataset whose licence bars commercial use.
